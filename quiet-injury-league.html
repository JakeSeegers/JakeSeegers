How the score is weighted

The weights follow one rule: the more power someone has to hide or show a danger, the more their silence counts.

  • Harm points, from the NFL's own injury report. IR or PUP = 3 (out for at least 4 games). Out = 2. Doubtful = 1.5. Questionable = 1. A team's harm is the total for all its injured players.
  • Concussions and head injuries count ×2. For decades the league played down concussions while former players developed CTE, a brain disease linked to repeated hits to the head. It is the clearest case of a danger that was hidden.
  • No body part listed counts ×1.5. Teams can keep injury details vague. An injury with no detail is harder for anyone to follow.
  • News media: 40%. Teams, the league and the press decide which injuries become stories. They have the most power to show a danger, so their silence counts the most.
  • The permanent record (Wikipedia): 35%. What goes into the record is what history keeps. An injury left out of it is easy to forget.
  • Fan attention (Wikipedia spike): 25%. Fans react to what they are shown, and much of their attention follows fantasy points. They count, but they have the least power.

Each part ranks the 32 teams from 0 (most attention for its harm) to 100 (least). The Silence Score is the weighted average of the three ranks. If a part has no data for a team, the other parts share its weight.

Where the numbers come from

  • Injuries: the current NFL injury list from the Sleeper API. A player counts if his status is IR, PUP, Out, Doubtful or Questionable.
  • Extra views: how many more people than normal read the player's English Wikipedia article in the 30 days after the injury. "Normal" is his median daily views in the 30 days before it. When Sleeper gives no injury date, the app uses the date of his last news update ("approx.") or the last 30 days ("date unknown"). Data from the Wikimedia Pageviews API.
  • On Wikipedia: whether the article mentions an injury in the current season. "Yes" means it names this body part. "Some" means it mentions an injury but not this body part.
  • Headlines: English news headlines in the last 30 days that name the player, from articles that mention an injury. Data from the GDELT news database. GDELT allows one request every 5 seconds, so this column fills in over a few minutes.

Fans look up a star quarterback's ankle hundreds of thousands of times. A backup lineman with a concussion gets almost no attention. The harm is the same kind of harm. The attention is not. If a team is winning this league, that is the problem.
